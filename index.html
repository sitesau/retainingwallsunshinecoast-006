Retaining Walls Sunshine Coast Explained

Retaining Wall Sunshine Coast frames the first decision around height, length, what sits above the wall and the suburb or postcode, because those details shape cost, approvals, drainage and contractor fit. A low garden terrace has a different risk profile from a wall supporting a driveway, boundary fill or steep change in level.

The published guide is clear that a wall on the Blackall Range at Maleny and a wall on canal fill at Maroochydore should not be treated as the same job. Basalt clay, volcanic soil, sandstone and coastal dune sand are named because ground behaviour changes what a sensible wall looks like. That is more useful than choosing a material first and hoping the site agrees.

  • Measure height and length so the wall face area can be calculated.
  • Describe what sits above the wall, such as a garden bed, driveway or building load.
  • Ask whether the wall is new work, repair, replacement or modification.
  • Confirm how water will be collected and discharged behind the wall.

Cost bands and what they leave out

The source guide prices retaining work by square metre of wall face, not by lineal metre. That distinction matters when comparing quotes, because a 10 metre wall can have very different cost depending on whether it is 600 millimetres high or 1.2 metres high. The guide gives indicative face-area bands only, and it names several exclusions.

Rock and boulder walls are listed at about $150 to $300 per square metre, timber sleepers at $300 to $450, gabion at $250 to $430, concrete sleepers at $550 to $700 and core-filled block at $550 to $750. Those bands exclude GST, excavation, drainage, imported backfill, engineering and certification. Treat them as a screening tool for early conversations, then ask for a written scope that separates wall structure, drainage, access, spoil, certification and finish.

Drainage is part of the structure

The guide repeatedly treats subsoil drainage as structural, not decorative. It names ag drain, aggregate and geofabric as part of the scope discussed behind retaining walls, and describes poor drainage as the leading cause of wall failure. For a property owner, the quote question should be direct: where does water go, what sits behind the face and how is the drainage protected from clogging?

This is also where Sunshine Coast ground differences become practical rather than cosmetic. Wet hinterland slopes and coastal sand are both mentioned in the source, but they create different drainage conversations. A quote that only names the visible sleeper, block, rock or basket material is incomplete if it does not explain the hidden drainage layer and backfill assumptions.

Material choice follows site constraints

The named options are concrete sleeper, timber sleeper, besser block, rock and boulder, gabion, drainage systems, engineered walls and repair or replacement assessment. Concrete sleepers between galvanised posts are described as common for replacement work. Timber sleeper terraces are positioned for low garden falls. Core-filled besser block is tied to taller engineered and rendered jobs.

Rock walls are described as battered back into the slope and suited to acreage where there is room behind the face. Gabion baskets are described as strong, free-draining structures suited to sloping and erosion-prone sites. For another crawlable guide on the same topic, see this Sunshine Coast retaining wall planning guide. The useful comparison is not which material is best in the abstract, but which one matches height, room behind the face, finish expectations, drainage and approval needs.

Who this applies to

This guide applies to owners checking a new wall, a failing wall, a replacement wall or a wall that may need engineering or council approval. The source page says Sunshine Coast Council requires a building permit before construction, modification or repair of most retaining walls, and that council concurrence applies for walls over 2 metres. It also says walls over 1 metre or carrying a surcharge can sit on an engineered pathway using AS 4678, Form 15 and Form 16.

That makes the early brief important. A quote request should include measurements, site access, slope, what sits above the wall and whether the work is near a boundary or existing structure. Where licensing is raised, the source states that QBCC licensing is triggered by the value of building work, not by wall height.

  1. Measure the wall face. Record height and length so quotes can be compared by square metre of face.
  2. Describe the load. Note what sits above the wall, because surcharge affects design and approval questions.
  3. Ask about drainage. Request the ag drain, aggregate, geofabric and discharge assumptions in writing.
  4. Check approvals. Ask whether the work needs a building permit, council concurrence or engineering certification.

Common questions

Why is wall face area used instead of length? The source guide calculates cost from height multiplied by length. A 1.2 metre high and 10 metre long wall is 12 square metres of face, not simply 10 metres of wall.

Which retaining wall material is cheapest? The published indicative bands put rock and boulder walls at the lower end and core-filled block at the higher end. The guide also says exclusions such as excavation, drainage, imported backfill, engineering and certification can change the final quote.

When does engineering come into the discussion? The source page names walls over 1 metre or walls carrying a surcharge as part of an engineered pathway. It refers to AS 4678 design, Form 15 and Form 16 certification.

Does every wall need drainage? The source guide treats subsoil drainage as a requirement behind every wall it discusses. It names ag drain, aggregate and geofabric as part of the drainage scope.
